## 1. Model

Your dilemma "probabilities or a neural network" skips the middle option: gradient boosting on tabular features. That is what I recommend.

| | Probabilistic algorithm | Boosting (LightGBM) | Neural network (GRU/TCN) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Implementation | frequency tables, conditional probabilities | features + `fit`, minutes on a CPU | sequence windows, PyTorch, tuning |
| Pros | transparent, fast | the best on tabular data, shows feature importance | learns temporal patterns by itself |
| Cons | does not catch interactions between oblasts | we invent the features by hand | few events, attack patterns change, in 2 days it is easy to lose to boosting |

Alert starts in Kyiv over 4.5 years are on the order of a few thousand (an estimate, I will check on the data). That is little for a neural network. I propose logistic regression + LightGBM, and a neural network only if time remains.

**Two separate tasks** (your Yes/No decision is preserved):
- **Start:** no alert → will one start within H min (H = 30).
- **End:** an alert is on → will it end within H min.

They must be separated. In the [arXiv paper 2411.14625](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.14625v1) the target is "alert active in 5 min", and there the model guesses right mostly because the alert simply continues. The same paper confirms your hypothesis: neighbouring oblasts are the most important features.

**Metrics:**
- **PR-AUC** — the main one, because the classes are imbalanced.
- **Brier score + calibration curve** — because we show a probability.
- **Precision / recall / F1** at a threshold picked on validation (the Yes/No is taken from it).
- **False "Yes" per day** — an operational metric.

**Baselines:**
1. Climatology: frequency of an alert start by hour of day × day of week.
2. Neighbour rule: "Yes" if there is an alert right now in Kyiv oblast or an adjacent one.
3. Logistic regression only on the current state of oblasts.

For the "end" task the baseline is the empirical duration curve: P(end within 30 min | the alert has already lasted t min).

**Split:** strictly chronological, no shuffling.
- Train: 2022-03-15 … 2026-03-31
- Validation: 2026-04-01 … 2026-06-30 (threshold, hyperparameters)
- Test: 2026-07-01 … today (we look once at the end)
- A gap ≥ H between the parts, so that the target does not leak across the boundary.

We will fix the dates finally after EDA: old years may hurt, because the nature of the attacks has changed.

## Ubilling: the format

The address is `https://ubilling.net.ua/aerialalerts/`, no token is needed, the limit is 2 requests/s, the cache on their side is 3 s. There are two modes.

**Simple** (no parameters) — 26 regions, two fields for each:
```json
"м. Київ": {"alertnow": true, "changed": "2026-10-10 22:24:47"}
```
- `alertnow` — there is an alert. For an oblast this already means "at least one raion": in Chernihiv oblast the oblast flag is off, there is an alert in 2 of 5 raions, `alertnow` = true. It matches your rule.
- `changed` — the time of the last change, Kyiv time. For most oblasts there is a placeholder `1970-01-01` there, that is, no start time.

**Raw** (`?raw`) — the same, but with raions, time and level:
```json
"м. Київ": {"enabled": true, "enabled_at": "2026-10-10T19:24:47.804Z", "alert_level": "yellow", "districts": []}
```
- `enabled_at` — the start of the alert, UTC, as in the dataset.
- `alert_level` — `yellow` or `red`. In the response right now: 21 raions red, 16 yellow; Kyiv — yellow.
- `districts` — the same for every raion of the oblast.

**We take `?raw`.** It closes both problems: it gives the start time of the neighbours' alerts (needed for features like "how many minutes there has been an alert in Chernihiv oblast") and the level. The yellow/red problem can now be not only mentioned: the level can be shown in the console. The model is still trained on data without levels, and this stays in the README as a limitation.

The `source=` parameter switches the upstream source, and the sources do not agree with each other: in the same minute one showed 12 active regions, the others 11, 6 and 2. We keep the default (Vadym Klymenko) — the same author as our dataset.

## Options

**A. Store and compute in UTC, calendar features in Kyiv time** (recommended)
- We do not touch the database. Durations, lags, the target "within 30 min" are computed in UTC, where a day is always 24 hours.
- Hour of day and day of week are computed with `zoneinfo` (`Europe/Kyiv`) — the standard library itself knows the switch dates for all years.
- Minus: two representations of time in the code; a test for the switch days is needed.

**B. Convert the whole database to Kyiv time on loading**
- What you proposed. Plus: everything in one time that a person understands.
- Minus: in autumn the hour 03:00–04:00 occurs twice, in spring 03:00–04:00 does not exist. An alert across the switch will get a duration an hour longer or shorter, sorting breaks on that night. To fix this the offset has to be stored next to every timestamp — that is, arriving at option A by a harder road.

**C. Everything in UTC, plus a feature "summer time: yes/no"**
- The simplest, no conversions. LightGBM can learn the one-hour shift itself.
- Minus: the climatology baseline and the probabilistic algorithm will not learn it, their "hour → frequency" tables will be smeared by an hour. The comparison of models becomes unfair.

**D. A fixed offset of +2 or +3 all year**
- No double and no missing hours.
- Minus: for half the year the hour is wrong by one. Worse than A with no advantage.

A separate nuance: Russia has no switch to summer time (Moscow is UTC+3 all year). If attacks are planned by Moscow time, then the "right" clock is not the Kyiv one. The table above speaks rather for the Kyiv one, but weakly. In option A this is cheap to check: give LightGBM both features (Kyiv hour and UTC hour) and see in EDA which is more stable.

Option A in one sentence: UTC is the "ruler" with which we measure everything related to duration and the order of events, and Kyiv time is a "label" that we hang on every point so that the model knows what hour it is for people. The database is not rewritten.

## The algorithm step by step

**1. Loading.** We read the CSV, remove full duplicates, leave `started_at` and `finished_at` as they are — in UTC. No conversion at this step.

**2. Time grid in UTC.** We build regular steps (for example, every minute or every 5 min) from 2022-03-15 to 2026-09-07. There are no switches in UTC, so every day is exactly 24 hours, and neighbouring points are always the same distance apart.

**3. State of regions on the grid — in UTC.** For every point `t` and every oblast: is there an alert or not (the "at least one raion" rule).

**4. Features that depend on intervals — in UTC:**
- how many minutes the alert in Chernihiv oblast has already lasted;
- how many minutes have passed since the last alert in Kyiv;
- how many neighbouring oblasts are under alert right now.

**5. The target — in UTC.** "Will an alert start in Kyiv in the interval (t, t+30 min]". 30 minutes is always 30 minutes.

**6. Calendar features — in Kyiv time.** Only here does a conversion appear: for every point `t` we compute `t` in the `Europe/Kyiv` zone and take the hour of day and the day of week from it. These are new columns alongside, they replace nothing. The `zoneinfo` library itself knows that it is +3 in summer and +2 in winter, and exactly when the switches were in each year.

**7. The train / validation / test split** — by UTC timestamps.

**8. Live operation.** Ubilling gives `enabled_at` in UTC → the features are computed by the same code as in training. The hour for the model — likewise through `Europe/Kyiv`. On the screen we show Kyiv time.

## An example on a real conversion

The same UTC hour, different seasons:

| Timestamp in the database (UTC) | Kyiv time | Hour for the model | Day of week |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2025-07-15 21:30 | 16.07, 00:30 (+3) | 0 | Wednesday |
| 2025-12-15 21:30 | 15.12, 23:30 (+2) | 23 | Monday |

Two effects are visible here: in winter the same UTC hour is a different hour for people, and in summer a late UTC evening is already the next day of the week in Kyiv. If the hour and the day are taken straight from UTC, both will be wrong.

## What happens on a switch night

The switch is always at 01:00 UTC. I checked all 9 switches in our period:
- **Spring:** after 02:59:59 comes 04:00:00 straight away. There is no hour "3" on that day, the day has 23 hours.
- **Autumn:** after 03:59:59 comes 03:00:00 again. The hour "3" occurs twice, the day has 25 hours.

In option A this breaks nothing: the UTC grid runs evenly; in autumn two neighbouring UTC hours simply get the same label "hour 3", and in spring the label "3" is skipped. Durations and the target do not notice it.

How to read it:

- **Alerts warned** — the share of alerts before which the program said "yes" at least once within 30 min. This is closer to what the user will feel, and it is always higher than recall by points.

## Item 10: the method, waiting for confirmation

**What we estimate.** How far the differences on the test may be an accident of which days were chosen.

**The method — a paired block bootstrap by days:**
1. I cut the test period into 68 day-blocks. The day boundary is 12:00 Kyiv time, not midnight, so that a night attack stays whole in one block.
2. 2,000 times I draw 68 blocks with replacement and compute the metrics on each draw.
3. Both models being compared are computed on the same draw of blocks, so the difference between them is estimated directly.
4. I report a 95% interval (the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles) and the share of draws in which the difference is above zero.

Blocks are needed because neighbouring 5-minute points are almost identical; resampling separate points would give falsely narrow intervals.

**What exactly I compute:**
- "Start", PR-AUC: LightGBM, logistic regression and the difference between them — in both modes; plus the difference between the modes for LightGBM.
- "End", precision of "yes" at the frozen threshold: LightGBM, the duration curve and the difference — in both modes.

**The rule of the conclusion, in advance:** if the 95% interval of a difference contains zero, I write "not distinguishable on this test". I rewrite decision 2 (item 11) according to this result, whatever it turns out to be.

**One problem I must name.** You asked for "only resampling of forecasts already computed". The forecasts on the test were not saved to disk. I will load LightGBM and the frequency tables from the saved files — without training. But the logistic regression and the duration curve were not saved to files, so they will have to be trained once more by the same procedure as in `scripts/final.py`. They are deterministic; before the bootstrap the script will check that their metrics match the published ones to the digit (17.1%, 19.4%, 52.0%) and will stop if not. Nothing is tuned and the thresholds are the same.

**What the method will not show:**
- The spread from the training of the model itself and from a change in the regime of attacks — only the accident of which days were chosen.
- 68 blocks is few, and attack waves last several days, so the days are not fully independent. The intervals will be too narrow rather than too wide.

## The reason: three links

**1. The threshold rule put the two models at very different points.** The rule from decision 21: among thresholds with a precision ≥ 60% on validation, take the one that catches the most endings. LightGBM ranked much better on validation, so it could afford a far more permissive threshold and still show 60% there.

| | Threshold | Validation: precision / says "yes" | Test: precision / says "yes" |
|---|---|---|---|
| LightGBM | 0.223 | 60.0% / 68% | 45.2% / 88% |
| Duration curve | 0.393 | 60.7% / 54% | 52.0% / 49% |

That is, the rule rewarded the better validation result with a threshold close to "always yes". An answer that says "yes" 88% of the time cannot be noticeably more precise than the 41% base rate, whatever the model.

**2. On the test LightGBM's probabilities turned out too high.** The mean forecast is 54.8% against 41.1% actual. Because of this the share of moments above the threshold grew from 68% to 88%. For the duration curve it is the other way round: 37.2% against 41.1%, the share above the threshold 54% → 49%.

**3. The regularity that LightGBM had learned stopped holding.** Its main features are how long ago a new alert started in Chernihiv and Poltava oblasts (30% of the model's contribution together). The chance that the Kyiv alert ends within 30 min:

| Situation | Training | Validation | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| There is no alert in Chernihiv oblast | 66.9% | 71.3% | 27.4% |
| A new alert in Chernihiv oblast started < 30 min ago | 79.5% | 71.3% | 54.3% |
| The Kyiv alert has lasted < 15 min (the curve's input) | 51.8% | 61.4% | 54.6% |
| The Kyiv alert has lasted > 120 min (the curve's input) | 18.6% | 26.0% | 22.8% |

In training and validation "it is quiet in Chernihiv oblast" meant a quick all-clear in 67–71% of cases, on the test — in 27%. The model kept giving a high probability in these situations. The only input of the duration curve stayed almost stable.

If LightGBM's threshold is moved so that it says "yes" exactly as often as the curve (49% of alert time), its precision is 50.6% against 52.0%. The difference is −1.4 points, 95% interval −12.1 … +9.2 — the models are not distinguishable. This shift needs only the forecasts, without the answers, so it is not fitting; but it is also not the program we froze.
